The **negative side** is more convincing.

Here’s why:

- The affirmative argument tries to defend terrorism by focusing on **indirect or accidental benefits** like reforms, cooperation, and awareness. But these are speculative and do not follow reliably from terrorism itself.
- The negative side directly addresses the **core reality of terrorism**: it involves intentional violence against innocents, causing death, trauma, fear, and social instability. That is a much stronger and more concrete argument.
- The affirmative claims terrorism can force governments to confront oppression, but the negative correctly points out that this can just as easily lead to **authoritarian crackdowns**, surveillance, and further repression rather than genuine reform.
- The claim that terrorism promotes international unity is also weakened by the negative’s counterpoint that it often produces **retaliation, escalation, and deeper conflict** instead.
- Most importantly, the negative offers a stronger moral and practical rebuttal: **nonviolent movements have historically achieved reform without terror**, so terrorism is neither necessary nor superior as a path to change.

Overall, the affirmative argument depends on unintended downstream effects, while the negative shows that terrorism’s direct harms are severe and its supposed benefits are uncertain, indirect, and often outweighed by backlash. Therefore, the **negative side wins**.
